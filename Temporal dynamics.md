Daryl Bem, a social psychologist, conducted a series of studies in the 2010s that he claimed provided evidence for precognition, or the ability to perceive future events before they occur. His work was published in the prestigious Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2011, sparking significant debate and controversy. Here's an overview of his studies, findings, and the criticisms they faced:


---

The Premonition Studies

Bem's research involved nine experiments with over 1,000 participants, designed to test whether future events could influence present behavior. Here are some notable examples:

1. Precognitive Detection of Erotic Stimuli

Participants were shown two curtains on a screen. Behind one was an erotic image, and behind the other, a blank space.

The position of the image was determined randomly after the participant made their choice.

Bem reported that participants chose the correct curtain slightly more often than chance would predict (~53% vs. 50%).



2. Retroactive Priming

Participants were shown a target word and asked to judge whether it was positive or negative.

After their judgment, a randomly selected "prime" word appeared, designed to either match or contradict the target word’s emotional tone.

Bem claimed that the prime word influenced participants' earlier judgments, even though it appeared only after they made their decisions.



3. Retroactive Practice Effect

Participants practiced recalling a list of words after a memory test. Bem found that participants remembered practiced words better than unpracticed ones, even though the practice happened after the test.





---

Findings

Bem’s results consistently suggested small but statistically significant effects indicating precognition. His conclusion: humans might have a capacity to "sense" future events, particularly those with emotional significance.


---

Why the Results Were Controversial

1. Violation of Established Scientific Principles

Bem’s claims challenged the mainstream understanding of causality and time, as they implied effects could precede causes—a concept with no grounding in current scientific theory.



2. Statistical Concerns

Critics argued that Bem used questionable statistical methods, including:

P-hacking: Selectively analyzing data to achieve statistically significant results.

Post-hoc Hypothesis Testing: Formulating hypotheses after observing the data, which can inflate the likelihood of false positives.




3. Replication Failures

Numerous researchers attempted to replicate Bem’s findings but failed to reproduce his results. The replication crisis in psychology, which highlighted widespread issues with experimental rigor, cast further doubt on his work.



4. Publication Bias

Critics pointed out that positive findings are more likely to be published than negative ones. Bem’s paper might have been published because of its sensational nature, despite weak evidence.



5. Extraordinary Claims Require Extraordinary Evidence

Bem’s conclusions would require a paradigm shift in physics and psychology. Many scientists felt that his evidence fell far short of the rigor needed to justify such claims.





---

Defending Bem

To his credit, Bem openly shared his methods and data, encouraging replication efforts. He argued that the failure of replications could stem from subtle methodological differences or experimenter bias.

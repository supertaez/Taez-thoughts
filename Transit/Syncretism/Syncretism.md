The gods [Persephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persephone "Persephone")\- [Isis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isis "Isis") and [Hades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hades_(mythology) "Hades (mythology)")\- [Serapis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serapis "Serapis"), an example of [Greco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_religion "Ancient Greek religion")\- [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion "Ancient Egyptian religion") syncretism

**Syncretism** ()[\[1\]](#cite_note-1) is the practice of combining different beliefs and various [schools of thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_thought "School of thought"). Syncretism involves the merging or [assimilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_assimilation "Religious assimilation") of several originally discrete [traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tradition "Tradition"), especially in the [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology") and [mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythology "Mythology") of [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion"), thus asserting an underlying unity and allowing for an [inclusive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusivism "Inclusivism") approach to other faiths. While syncretism in [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art "Art") and [culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture "Culture") is sometimes likened to [eclecticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclecticism "Eclecticism"), in the realm of religion, it specifically denotes a more integrated merging of beliefs into a unified system, distinct from eclecticism, which implies a selective adoption of elements from different traditions without necessarily blending them into a new, cohesive belief system. Syncretism also manifests in [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics "Politics"), known as [syncretic politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretic_politics "Syncretic politics").

The English word is first attested in the early 17th century [\[2\]](#cite_note-2). It is from [Modern Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Latin "Neo-Latin") _syncretismus_, drawing on the συγκρητισμός (synkretismos), supposedly meaning "Cretan federation." However, this is a spurious etymology derived from the naive idea in [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch "Plutarch")'s 1st-century AD essay on "Fraternal Love (Peri Philadelphias)" in his collection _[Moralia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moralia "Moralia")_. He cites the example of the [Cretans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Crete "History of Crete"), who compromised and reconciled their differences and came together in alliance when faced with external dangers. "And that is their so-called _Syncretism_ \[Union of Cretans\]." A more likely etymology is from _sun-_ ("with") plus _kerannumi_ ("mix") and its related noun, _krasis_ ("mixture").

## Social and political roles

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Syncretism&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Social and political roles")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2c/Elephant-shaped_column_brackets_at_Lahore_Fort.jpg/170px-Elephant-shaped_column_brackets_at_Lahore_Fort.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Elephant-shaped_column_brackets_at_Lahore_Fort.jpg)

The use of elephant-shaped column brackets in buildings of the [Lahore Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lahore_Fort "Lahore Fort") reflects [Hindu influences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_temple_architecture "Hindu temple architecture") on Mughal Architecture during the reign of [Akbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar "Akbar"). Islam forbids [representation of living figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aniconism_in_Islam "Aniconism in Islam").

Overt syncretism in folk belief may show cultural acceptance of an alien or previous tradition, but the "other" cult may survive or infiltrate without authorized _syncresis_. For example, some [conversos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversos "Conversos") developed a sort of [cult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice) "Cult (religious practice)") for martyr-victims of the [Spanish Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Inquisition "Spanish Inquisition"), thus incorporating elements of [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism "Catholicism") while resisting it.

The [Kushite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushite "Kushite") kings who ruled Upper Egypt for approximately a century and the whole of Egypt for approximately 57 years, from 721 to 664 BCE, constituting the [Twenty-fifth Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-fifth_Dynasty_of_Egypt "Twenty-fifth Dynasty of Egypt") in [Manetho's _Aegyptiaca_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manetho#Aegyptiaca "Manetho"), developed a syncretic worship identifying their own god [Dedun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedun "Dedun") with the Egyptian [Osiris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osiris "Osiris"). They maintained that worship even after they had been driven out of Egypt. A temple dedicated to this syncretic god, built by the [Kushite ruler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush "Kingdom of Kush") [Atlanersa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlanersa "Atlanersa"), was unearthed at [Jebel Barkal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jebel_Barkal "Jebel Barkal").[\[3\]](#cite_note-3)[\[4\]](#cite_note-4)

Syncretism was common during the [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic "Hellenistic") period, with rulers regularly identifying local deities in various parts of their domains with the relevant god or goddess of the [Greek Pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Pantheon "Greek Pantheon") as a means of increasing the cohesion of their kingdom. This practice was accepted in most locations but vehemently rejected by the [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews"), who considered the identification of [Yahweh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Judaism "God in Judaism") with the Greek [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus "Zeus") as the worst of blasphemy.

The [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") continued the practice, first by the identification of traditional Roman deities with Greek ones, producing a single [Greco-Roman pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mythology "Classical mythology"), and then identifying members of that pantheon with the local deities of various Roman provinces.

Some religious movements have embraced overt syncretism, such as the case of [melding Shintō beliefs into Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinbutsu-sh%C5%ABg%C5%8D "Shinbutsu-shūgō") or the amalgamation of Germanic and Celtic [pagan views](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_Paganism#Pagan_influences_on_Christianity "Christianity and Paganism") into [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christianity#Spread_of_Christianity "Early Christianity") during its spread into Gaul, Ireland, Britain, Germany and Scandinavia. In later times, Christian missionaries in [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America "North America") identified [Manitou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manitou "Manitou"), the spiritual and fundamental life force in the traditional beliefs of the [Algonquian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algonquian_people "Algonquian people"), with the [God of Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_of_Christianity "God of Christianity"). Similar identifications were made by missionaries at other locations in the Americas and Africa who encountered a local belief in a Supreme God or Supreme Spirit of some kind.

Indian influences are seen in the practice of [Shi'i Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi%27i_Islam "Shi'i Islam") in [Trinidad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinidad "Trinidad"). Others have strongly rejected it as devaluing and compromising precious and genuine distinctions; examples include post-[Exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity_of_Judah "Babylonian captivity of Judah") [Second Temple Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Temple_Judaism "Second Temple Judaism"), [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam"), and most of [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism "Protestantism") Christianity.\[_[further explanation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_\]\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]

Syncretism tends to facilitate coexistence and unity between otherwise different cultures and world views ([intercultural competence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercultural_competence "Intercultural competence")), a factor that has recommended it to rulers of multiethnic [realms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire "Empire"). Conversely, the rejection of syncretism, usually in the name of "[piety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piety "Piety")" and "[orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodoxy "Orthodoxy")", may help to generate, bolster or authenticate a sense of uncompromised [cultural unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_identity "Cultural identity") in a well-defined minority or majority.

All major religious conversions of populations have had elements from prior religious traditions incorporated into legends or doctrine that endure with the newly converted [laity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laity "Laity").[\[5\]](#cite_note-5)

## Religious syncretism

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Syncretism&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Religious syncretism")\]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ea/Statue_of_the_god_Anubis.jpg/180px-Statue_of_the_god_Anubis.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Statue_of_the_god_Anubis.jpg)

The god [Hermanubis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermanubis "Hermanubis"), an example of [Greco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_religion "Ancient Greek religion")\- [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion "Ancient Egyptian religion") syncretism

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/da/Taranis_Jupiter_with_wheel_and_thunderbolt_Le_Chatelet_Gourzon_Haute_Marne.jpg/130px-Taranis_Jupiter_with_wheel_and_thunderbolt_Le_Chatelet_Gourzon_Haute_Marne.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Taranis_Jupiter_with_wheel_and_thunderbolt_Le_Chatelet_Gourzon_Haute_Marne.jpg)

The god [Taranis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taranis "Taranis")\- [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_(mythology) "Jupiter (mythology)"), an example of [Romano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_religion "Ancient Roman religion")\- [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Celtic_religion "Ancient Celtic religion") syncretism

Religious syncretism is the blending of two or more religious [belief systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief#Collective_belief "Belief") into a new system, or the incorporation into a religious tradition of beliefs from unrelated traditions. This can occur for many reasons, and the latter scenario happens quite commonly in areas where multiple religious traditions exist in proximity and function actively in a culture, or when a culture is conquered, and the conquerors bring their religious beliefs with them, but do not succeed in entirely eradicating the old beliefs or (especially) practices.

Religions may have syncretic elements to their beliefs or history, but adherents of so-labeled systems often frown on applying the label, especially adherents who belong to "revealed" religious systems, such as the [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions"), or any system that exhibits an [exclusivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusivism "Exclusivism") approach. Such adherents sometimes see syncretism as a betrayal of their pure truth. By this reasoning, adding an incompatible belief corrupts the original religion, rendering it no longer true. Indeed, critics of a syncretistic trend may use the word or its variants as a disparaging epithet, as a charge implying that those who seek to incorporate a new view, belief, or practice into a religious system pervert the original faith. Non-exclusivist systems of belief, on the other hand, may feel quite free to incorporate other traditions into their own. Keith Ferdinando notes that the term "syncretism" is an elusive one,[\[6\]](#cite_note-6) and can refer to substitution or modification of the central elements of a religion by beliefs or practices introduced from elsewhere. The consequence under such a definition, according to Ferdinando, can lead to a fatal "compromise" of the original religion's "integrity".[\[7\]](#cite_note-7)

In modern [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism "Secularism") society, religious innovators sometimes construct new faiths or key tenets syncretically, with the added benefit or aim of reducing inter-religious discord. Such chapters often have a side-effect of arousing jealousy and suspicion among authorities and ardent adherents of the pre-existing religion. Such religions tend to inherently appeal to an inclusive, diverse audience. Sometimes the state itself sponsored such new movements, such as the [Living Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_Church "Living Church") founded in [Soviet Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Russia "Soviet Russia") and the [German Evangelical Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Evangelical_Church "German Evangelical Church") in [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany"), chiefly to stem all outside influences.

## Cultures and societies

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Syncretism&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Cultures and societies")\]

According to some authors, "Syncretism is often used to describe the product of the large-scale imposition of one alien culture, religion, or body of practices over another that is already present."[\[8\]](#cite_note-8) Others such as [Jerry H. Bentley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_H._Bentley "Jerry H. Bentley"), however, have argued that syncretism has also helped to create cultural compromise. It provides an opportunity to bring beliefs, values, and customs from one cultural tradition into contact with, and to engage different cultural traditions. Such a migration of ideas is generally successful only when there is a resonance between both traditions. While, as Bentley has argued, there are numerous cases where expansive traditions have won popular support in foreign lands, this is not always so.[\[9\]](#cite_note-9)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/Jesuits_at_Akbar%27s_court.jpg/170px-Jesuits_at_Akbar%27s_court.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Jesuits_at_Akbar%27s_court.jpg)

Akbar the Great holding a court discussing theology

In the 16th century, the [Mughal emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire "Mughal Empire") [Akbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar "Akbar") proposed a new religion called the [Din-i Ilahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Din-i_Ilahi "Din-i Ilahi") ("Divine Faith"). Sources disagree with respect to whether it was one of many Sufi orders or merged some of the elements of the various religions of his empire.[\[10\]](#cite_note-10)[\[11\]](#cite_note-11) Din-i Ilahi drew elements primarily from [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") and [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism") but also from [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"), [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism "Jainism"), and [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism"). More resembling a [personality cult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_cult "Personality cult") than a religion, it had no sacred scriptures, no priestly hierarchy, and fewer than 20 disciples, all hand-picked by Akbar himself. It is also accepted that the policy of _sulh-i-kul_, which formed the essence of the Dīn-i Ilāhī, was adopted by Akbar as a part of general imperial administrative policy. _Sulh-i-kul_ means "universal peace".[\[12\]](#cite_note-12)[\[13\]](#cite_note-13)

The syncretic [deism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deism "Deism") of [Matthew Tindal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Tindal "Matthew Tindal") undermined Christianity's claim to uniqueness.[\[14\]](#cite_note-Harding_1995_p.-14) The modern, rational, non-pejorative connotations of syncretism arguably date from [Denis Diderot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Diderot "Denis Diderot")'s _[Encyclopédie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die "Encyclopédie")_ articles _Eclecticisme_ and _Syncrétistes, Hénotiques, ou Conciliateurs_. Diderot portrayed syncretism as the concordance of eclectic sources. Scientific or legalistic approaches of subjecting all claims to [critical thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_thinking "Critical thinking") prompted at this time much literature in Europe and the Americas studying non-European religions such as Edward Moor's _[The Hindu Pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hindu_Pantheon "The Hindu Pantheon")_ of 1810, much of which was almost evangelistically appreciative by embracing spirituality and creating the space and tolerance in particular [disestablishment of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disestablishmentarianism "Disestablishmentarianism") (or its stronger form, official secularisation as in France) whereby believers of [spiritualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritualism_(beliefs) "Spiritualism (beliefs)"), [agnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnosticism "Agnosticism"), [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism "Atheism") and in many cases more innovative or pre-Abrahimic based religions could promote and spread their belief system, whether in the family or beyond.\[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_\]
